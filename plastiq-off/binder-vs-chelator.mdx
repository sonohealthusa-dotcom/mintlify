A **detox binder** and a **chelator** are often confused, but they work in different places and are used for different reasons. A binder like **clinoptilolite zeolite** works inside the **digestive tract**, attaching to certain substances so they pass through the gut. A **chelator** is a specific agent — often a prescription drug — that binds metals in the **bloodstream** and is used medically under supervision.

## How a binder works

A binder such as clinoptilolite zeolite has a porous, negatively charged structure that traps certain positively charged compounds inside its cavities **within the digestive tract**, so they can leave in stool instead of being reabsorbed. Because it acts in the gut and isn't meaningfully absorbed, it's used as **gentle, general detox-pathway support** — not as a systemic treatment. This is the binder in [Plastiq Off](/plastiq-off/overview). For how it compares to other binders, see [detox binders compared](/plastiq-off/detox-binders-compared).

## How a chelator works

A chelator is typically a **prescription medication** (such as DMSA, DMPS, or EDTA) given under medical supervision to treat diagnosed heavy-metal poisoning. It binds specific metals **in the blood and tissues** so they can be excreted. Because it acts systemically and can also pull beneficial minerals, it requires monitoring and is not a wellness supplement.

## Why the distinction matters

Some products blur the line, implying a supplement can "chelate" metals out of your body. That's a claim regulators act on, and it isn't supported for zeolite supplements. Compliant, accurate language is the green flag: a binder **supports your body's natural detoxification pathways** in the gut; it does not chelate.
