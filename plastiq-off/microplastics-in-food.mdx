The biggest dietary sources of microplastics are **seafood, salt, bottled and tap water, and foods packaged or heated in plastic** — and the highest-impact way to cut exposure is to store and heat food in glass or steel instead of plastic. No food is "microplastic-free," but a few simple swaps meaningfully reduce how much you take in.

## Which foods carry the most microplastics?

Microplastics have been measured across many foods, but a few stand out:

- **Seafood**, especially shellfish like mussels, oysters, and clams — because you eat the whole animal, including the digestive tract where particles collect.
- **Salt**, particularly sea salt, which concentrates particles from ocean water.
- **Bottled water**, which a 2024 study found can carry hundreds of thousands of nanoplastic particles per liter.
- **Foods packaged, stored, or heated in plastic** — takeout containers, plastic-wrapped items, and anything microwaved in plastic.
- **Highly processed foods**, which pass through more plastic equipment and packaging on the way to you.

## How do microplastics get into food?

Two main routes: **environmental** (plastic pollution breaking down in oceans and soil, then entering the food chain) and **contact** (particles shedding directly from packaging, bottles, cutting boards, and containers — especially with heat, acidity, or wear). Contact sources are the ones you can most easily control.

## What actually helps

- **Store and reheat in glass or steel**, not plastic. Heat drives more particles into food and drink.
- **Skip single-use plastic water bottles** — refill glass or stainless steel. See [reduce microplastics in drinking water](/plastiq-off/reduce-microplastics-in-drinking-water).
- **Cook more whole foods** and cut back on heavily packaged, processed items.
- **Filter tap water** with a certified particulate filter.
- **Don't microwave in plastic**, and replace scratched plastic containers and worn cutting boards.
- **Eat fiber-rich foods**, which support your body's normal elimination.
