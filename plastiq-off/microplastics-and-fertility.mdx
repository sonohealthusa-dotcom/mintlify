Researchers have detected microplastics in human reproductive tissues, including semen, follicular fluid, and the placenta, and lab studies raise questions about effects on hormones and fertility — but the **human evidence is still emerging and far from settled**. The best-supported response today is reducing everyday exposure, not relying on any product to reverse it.

## What researchers have actually found

- **Microplastics have been detected in reproductive tissues** in several studies, showing exposure reaches these areas — but detection is not the same as proven harm.
- **Some lab and animal studies** suggest certain plastic-associated chemicals can act on hormone pathways (so-called endocrine disruptors).
- **Human outcome data is limited.** Scientists have not established that everyday microplastic exposure causes infertility, and much remains unknown.

## Practical, evidence-based steps

Because reducing exposure is the best-supported strategy, focus on the everyday sources you can control:

1. **Swap plastic for glass or steel** for food storage and water bottles, and never microwave food in plastic. See [microplastics in food](/plastiq-off/microplastics-in-food).
2. **Filter your drinking water** — a quality or reverse-osmosis filter reduces waterborne microplastics. See [reduce microplastics in drinking water](/plastiq-off/reduce-microplastics-in-drinking-water).
3. **Cut single-use plastics** and choose fresh over heavily packaged foods.
4. **Support your body's normal pathways** with fiber, hydration, and general healthy habits. See [how to reduce microplastics](/plastiq-off/how-to-reduce-microplastics).
