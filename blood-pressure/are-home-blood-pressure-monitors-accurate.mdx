## How accurate are home blood pressure monitors, really?

For most adults, an automatic upper-arm (oscillometric) monitor tracks blood pressure closely enough to guide care between visits. The [American Heart Association recommends an automatic, cuff-style, upper-arm monitor](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home) for home use precisely because these devices are reliable and repeatable when used correctly. What they are *not* is a substitute for a clinician's interpretation — they give you the numbers; your doctor gives you the diagnosis.

## What does "clinically validated" mean?

A validated monitor is one an independent body has tested against a reference standard and confirmed measures within tight limits. In the U.S., the [Blood Pressure Validated Device Listing (VDL)](https://www.validatebp.org/), run by the American Medical Association, lists devices reviewed for clinical accuracy. Not every budget monitor has gone through this voluntary review, so absence from the list doesn't prove a device is inaccurate — but choosing a validated model is the surest way to know a monitor meets the standard. Either way, the method that matters is the same automatic oscillometric measurement SonoHealth's [BPpro and BPMAX](/blood-pressure/bppro-vs-bpmax) use.

## Does a cheaper monitor mean a less accurate one?

No. Accuracy depends on the oscillometric method and your technique, not the price. A well-fitted upper-arm monitor used correctly gives consistent readings whether it costs $35 or $135 — the extra money in premium models mostly buys app syncing, larger memory, and extras, not precision. ## What makes a home reading falsely high?

Technique errors, not the device, cause most "bad" readings. According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/high-blood-pressure/measure/index.html) and AHA, each of these can add several points:

- A full bladder, or measuring right after caffeine, food, or exercise
- Talking or moving during the reading
- Crossed legs, or back and feet unsupported
- The arm below heart level, or the cuff over clothing
- The wrong [cuff size](/blood-pressure/cuff-size-and-accuracy) — too small reads high, too large reads low

Rest five minutes, sit with feet flat and back supported, put the cuff on a bare arm at heart level, and stay quiet. Our [how-to-measure guide](/blood-pressure/how-to-measure-at-home) has the full routine.

## Is it normal for the second reading to be lower than the first?

Yes. The first reading is often the highest, then numbers settle as you relax. That's why guidelines say to take two or three readings about a minute apart and record the **average** rather than a single number. If your averaged readings stay high across several days, that's the signal to share with your clinician — see [how often to check](/blood-pressure/how-often-to-check-blood-pressure-at-home).

## Are home monitors accurate for everyone?

Automatic monitors are accurate for most adults, but a few situations reduce precision. A very irregular pulse such as [atrial fibrillation](/blood-pressure/irregular-heartbeat-detection), markedly stiff arteries (common with age), or certain arrhythmias can throw off an oscillometric reading. If that's you, confirm important readings with your clinician and, where possible, choose a validated device. This is about the measurement method, not the brand.

## How do I check whether my own monitor is accurate?

Bring it to a checkup and compare a home reading to the clinic's **on the same arm, within a few minutes of each other**. Readings within roughly 5–10 mm Hg suggest your device is tracking well. Re-check every year or so, and if numbers ever look implausible, start with [troubleshooting](/blood-pressure/blood-pressure-monitor-troubleshooting) — it's usually cuff fit or posture, not a broken monitor.

## When to involve your doctor

Home readings support — never replace — professional care. Contact your doctor if your readings are consistently high or you see an irregular-heartbeat flag. Treat a reading at or above 180/120 with symptoms as a medical emergency — call 911.
