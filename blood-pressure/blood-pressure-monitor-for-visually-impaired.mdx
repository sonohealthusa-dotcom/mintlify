The best blood pressure monitor for a blind or visually impaired person is a **talking automatic upper-arm monitor** that announces the reading aloud, so it can be used independently without seeing the screen. SonoHealth's **BPMAX** is built for exactly this, with a talking voice function designed for elderly and legally blind users.

<Note>
A home monitor is a tracking tool, not a diagnosis. Share your readings with your clinician and never adjust medication based on home numbers alone.
</Note>

## How can a blind or low-vision person check blood pressure at home?

Use a monitor that speaks the result aloud. Rest for five minutes, fit the [adjustable cuff](/blood-pressure/cuff-size-and-accuracy) on a bare upper arm at heart level, sit with your back supported and feet flat, and press the single start button. The monitor inflates, measures, and then announces your systolic, diastolic, and pulse numbers — no screen-reading required. Taking two or three readings a minute apart and noting the average gives your doctor a reliable trend.

## Why a talking monitor instead of a screen reader or app?

A dedicated talking monitor announces the numbers directly, with no phone, pairing, or app screen to navigate — which is often simpler and more reliable for someone who can't see a display. App-based monitors can work, but they add steps that depend on a phone's accessibility setup. For day-to-day independence, a self-contained talking device is usually the easier path.

## Does the voice feature affect accuracy?

No. The voice only reads out the value the monitor already measured, so a talking model is exactly as accurate as a standard one. Accuracy depends on the measurement method and [technique](/blood-pressure/how-to-measure-at-home) — resting first, the [right cuff size](/blood-pressure/cuff-size-and-accuracy), the cuff at heart level, and averaging readings. See our [talking blood pressure monitor guide](/blood-pressure/talking-blood-pressure-monitor) for more.

## Why upper-arm rather than wrist for low-vision users?

Upper-arm monitors are [more accurate and less position-sensitive](/blood-pressure/upper-arm-vs-wrist) than wrist models, which must be held precisely at heart level — hard to judge without seeing the display. An upper-arm cuff sits in a fixed, repeatable spot, which makes independent readings more consistent for someone who can't see the screen.

## Tips for caregivers and family

Set the cuff up snugly on a bare arm, keep readings at the same times each day, and store a tidy [log the doctor can use](/blood-pressure/morning-vs-evening-readings). If the person also has limited dexterity, resting the arm on a table removes the need to hold it up. Consistency is what turns scattered numbers into something a clinician can act on.

## When to involve the doctor

Contact the doctor if readings are consistently high, if there's an irregular-heartbeat flag, or if the person feels dizzy on standing. Treat a reading at or above 180/120 with symptoms as a medical emergency — call 911.
