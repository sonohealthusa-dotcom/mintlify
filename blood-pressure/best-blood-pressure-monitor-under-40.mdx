<Note>
A home blood pressure monitor is a tracking tool, not a diagnosis. Hypertension is diagnosed and managed by a clinician using readings over time — never start, stop, or change medication based on home numbers alone.
</Note>

## What should a blood pressure monitor under $40 include?

At this price you should still expect the essentials that drive accuracy and everyday use: an **automatic upper-arm cuff** (the style [most clinicians recommend over wrist models](/blood-pressure/upper-arm-vs-wrist)), a display that shows **systolic, diastolic, and pulse**, **built-in memory** so you can follow a trend, and an **adjustable cuff** that fits most adult arms. What you generally trade away versus premium models is app syncing, multi-hundred-reading memory, and extras like AC adapters — not accuracy itself, which comes from the oscillometric method and [your technique](/blood-pressure/how-to-measure-at-home).

## Does a cheaper monitor mean a less accurate one?

No. Accuracy depends on the measurement method and technique, not the price tag. A well-fitted upper-arm monitor used correctly — rested first, seated properly, cuff at heart level, two or three readings averaged — gives consistent home readings whether it's a budget or a premium model. The most common cause of a "bad" reading at any price is a [cuff that doesn't fit](/blood-pressure/cuff-size-and-accuracy) or poor positioning, not the device.

## Is a budget monitor worth it, or should I spend more?

For most people tracking blood pressure at home, an accurate under-$40 upper-arm monitor is all you need. Spend more only if you specifically want a feature a budget model lacks — for example app/Bluetooth syncing to a phone, or a very large stored-reading history. If those aren't priorities, put your effort into [good measurement technique](/blood-pressure/how-to-measure-at-home) and sharing your readings with your doctor, which matter far more than price.

## When to involve your doctor

Home readings support — but never replace — professional care. Contact your doctor if your readings are consistently at or above 130/80, if you see an irregular-heartbeat flag, or if anything changes. Treat a reading at or above 180/120 with symptoms as a medical emergency — call 911.
