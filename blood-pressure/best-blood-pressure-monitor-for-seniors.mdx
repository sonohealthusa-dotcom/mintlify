The best blood pressure monitor for a senior is a **validated automatic upper-arm monitor with one-button operation, a large high-contrast display, a correctly sized cuff, and reading memory**. Everything else is a nice-to-have. This guide covers what matters and how SonoHealth's BPpro and BPMAX compare.

<Note>
A home monitor is a tracking tool, not a diagnosis. Older adults especially should share readings with their clinician and never adjust medication based on home numbers alone.
</Note>

## What features matter most for older adults?

Prioritize simplicity and readability. A **one-button automatic upper-arm** design means no fiddly setup; a **large, backlit display** makes the numbers easy to read; and **reading memory** turns single measurements into a trend the doctor can use. A [talking monitor](/blood-pressure/talking-blood-pressure-monitor) that reads results aloud can be the deciding feature for anyone with low vision — see our guide to a [monitor for blind and low-vision users](/blood-pressure/blood-pressure-monitor-for-visually-impaired).

## Why is cuff size the most important detail?

Cuff fit affects accuracy more than any feature: a cuff that's too small reads falsely high, one too large reads low. Measure the bare upper arm and confirm it falls within the monitor's cuff range before buying — our [cuff size and accuracy guide](/blood-pressure/cuff-size-and-accuracy) explains how. This matters more with age because arm size and skin change over time.

## Should a senior use upper-arm or wrist?

Upper-arm, in almost every case. [Upper-arm monitors](/blood-pressure/upper-arm-vs-wrist) are more accurate and less position-sensitive than wrist models, which older users can find fiddly to hold at heart level. Reserve wrist monitors for people who genuinely can't use an arm cuff.

## How can a caregiver make readings reliable?

Take readings at the same times each day, after five minutes of rest, with the cuff on a bare upper arm at heart level. Keep a tidy [log the doctor can use](/blood-pressure/morning-vs-evening-readings) and watch for irregular-heartbeat flags. Consistency is what makes the numbers meaningful — see our [seniors monitoring guide](/blood-pressure/monitoring-for-seniors) and [how often to check](/blood-pressure/how-often-to-check-blood-pressure-at-home) for more.

## When to involve the doctor

Contact the doctor if readings are consistently high, if there's an irregular-heartbeat flag, or if the person feels dizzy on standing. Treat a reading at or above 180/120 with symptoms as a medical emergency — call 911.
